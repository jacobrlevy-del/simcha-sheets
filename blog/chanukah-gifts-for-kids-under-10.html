Chanukah Gifts for Kids Under $10: Ideas for All 8 Nights of 2026

Eight nights of presents can add up quickly, especially with more than one child. The good news is that small gifts are part of what makes Chanukah fun. A new dreidel, a book at bedtime or a coupon for “stay up 30 minutes late” can feel as exciting to a six-year-old as something big. This year Chanukah runs from the evening of Friday 4 December to Saturday 12 December 2026, so here are low-cost ideas for each night, plus a few things to plan around the two Friday nights.

A note on prices: costs vary a lot between shops, countries and seasons. The ideas below are things that can often be found for under $10, or made for less. Check prices locally before you plan around any of them. We don’t use affiliate links, and nobody has paid to be mentioned here.

Gelt first: the oldest Chanukah gift

In many families, giving gelt (coins) to children is a much older Chanukah custom than giving presents, which became widespread more recently. Gelt is also the cheapest gift there is, and it can carry the most meaning:

  • Real coins: a handful of shiny coins, or one new note, given each night. Some families give a little more each night as the candles increase.
  • Chocolate gelt: great for dreidel games. If you keep kosher, check the label for a hechsher, and note whether it’s dairy or pareve if you’re serving it after a meat meal.
  • Gelt with a tzedakah share: many families ask children to put part of their gelt aside for tzedakah, and let them choose the cause. It turns the cheapest gift into a lesson in giving. Our kids’ activities guide has a “giving night” built around this.

Chanukah-themed gifts

  • A dreidel of their own: wooden, plastic or light-up. Pair it with our free Dreidel Game Night sheet and a bag of chocolate coins and you have a complete gift.
  • A dreidel-making kit: plain wooden or card dreidels with paint pens, so they can decorate their own.
  • Chanukah stickers and a blank notebook for a “Chanukah journal”: one drawing or sentence per night.
  • Window gel clings or stencils of menorahs and stars, for decorating near where the menorah stands.
  • A Chanukah cookie cutter set, with a promise of a baking afternoon together.
  • A home-made songsheet of Ma’oz Tzur and the songs your family sings, decorated by the kids and kept by the menorah.

Books and reading

  • A paperback picture book about Chanukah or Jewish life. Second-hand shops, school book fairs and shul or library sales are often the cheapest sources.
  • A first chapter book for a new reader, with a handwritten note inside the cover.
  • An activity book: puzzles, mazes or colouring, especially good for long car journeys to relatives.
  • A personalised bookmark the older siblings make for the younger ones.
  • A library outing coupon: “one trip to the library, you choose three books.” It costs nothing and many children love it.

Crafts and making

  • Beeswax sheets and wick for rolling simple candles together. Use them as decoration or with adult supervision, and check with your rabbi or family custom before using home-made candles in the menorah.
  • Air-dry clay for making a dreidel, a small dish or a model menorah (for display, not for lighting).
  • Fuse beads or friendship-bracelet thread for older children.
  • A new set of markers, gel pens or chalk. Unexciting to adults, but very popular with children.
  • A home-made building kit: crackers, icing and sweets in a box, to build an edible menorah or a model Beit HaMikdash together on a weeknight.

Small toys and games

  • A deck of cards or a small card game the whole family can play on Saturday night.
  • A puzzle with a few dozen pieces for younger kids, a few hundred for older ones.
  • Bubbles, a skipping rope, a bouncy ball or a frisbee for daytime play.
  • A torch (flashlight) is a nice “festival of lights” gift and handy for reading under the covers.
  • Glow sticks for a lights-themed night. Not for very young children, who may bite them.

Free gifts children often love most

Some of the gifts children remember for years cost nothing at all:

  • Coupons: “choose dinner”, “stay up 30 minutes late”, “one hour of a parent’s full attention”, “skip one chore”, “breakfast in bed”.
  • A letter from a parent or grandparent about what they love about the child, read aloud by the menorah.
  • A family recipe card, handwritten, with a promise to cook it together.
  • A “yes day” hour where the answer to reasonable requests is yes.
  • Passing on something special: a grandparent’s old dreidel, a book from a parent’s childhood, or a photo of their first Chanukah.
  • Their own job at lighting time: handing over the shamash, reading the blessings card, or leading the song.

Planning gifts around the two Friday nights

This year the first and eighth nights both fall on Friday, and the second night is on Saturday night after Shabbat. In homes that keep Shabbat, a few practical questions come up:

  • Money on Shabbat: money is generally not handled on Shabbat in observant homes, so real-coin gelt is usually given before Shabbat starts, or saved for Saturday night. Chocolate gelt is a food, so it isn’t affected in the same way.
  • Wrapping and unwrapping: tearing paper, cutting tape and opening some packaging raise questions on Shabbat. Many families give Friday-night gifts before candle-lighting, or use a gift bag or an unsealed box. Ask your rabbi what’s right for your family.
  • Toys and games: some toys, such as electronic or battery-operated ones, or crafts that involve cutting, colouring or building, aren’t used on Shabbat. Save those for a weeknight, and keep the Friday gifts to things like a book or chocolate.

The calmest approach is often to give Friday’s gift in the afternoon, before the menorah and Shabbat candles are lit. Our guide to lighting the menorah on Friday night explains the order, and the Chanukah 2026 dates guide has the full night-by-night chart.

Safety and allergies

  • Small parts, marbles and balloons are choking hazards for children under 3. Check the age label on every toy.
  • Keep button batteries and small magnets well away from young children; they are dangerous if swallowed.
  • Chocolate gelt and sweets can contain nuts, milk or other allergens. Check the label if a guest has allergies.
  • Candles, even unlit decorative ones, and matches are not toys. Keep them out of reach.

Keep track without the stress
