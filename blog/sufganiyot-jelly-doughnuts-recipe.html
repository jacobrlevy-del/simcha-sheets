Sufganiyot: Israeli Jelly Doughnuts for Chanukah, Dairy or Pareve

In Israel, sufganiyot start appearing in bakery windows weeks before Chanukah: soft, round yeast doughnuts, fried in oil, filled with jam and buried in powdered sugar. Like latkes, they are eaten on Chanukah because they’re cooked in oil, a reminder of the small jar of oil in the Temple that burned for eight days. The name is usually linked to the Hebrew word sfog, meaning sponge, which describes the texture you’re aiming for.

Home-made sufganiyot are easier than they look. You need a soft, enriched dough, two rises, and oil at the right temperature. This guide gives you one recipe with a dairy option and a pareve option, so you can serve them after either a dairy or a meat meal.

Ingredients (makes about 16)

  • 500 g (about 4 cups) bread flour or plain/all-purpose flour
  • 7 g (1 packet, 2¼ tsp) instant or active dry yeast
  • 60 g (about 5 tbsp) sugar
  • 1 tsp salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 240 ml (1 cup) lukewarm liquid: milk for dairy, or water or unsweetened oat or soy milk for pareve
  • 50 g (3½ tbsp) fat: soft butter for dairy, or pareve margarine or 3 tbsp neutral oil for pareve
  • 1 tsp vanilla extract or grated lemon zest (optional)
  • Neutral oil for deep frying, such as sunflower, canola or peanut
  • About 300 g (1 cup) smooth jam, and powdered (icing) sugar for dusting

Bread flour gives a slightly chewier, sturdier doughnut; all-purpose flour gives a softer one. Both work. If you use active dry yeast rather than instant, stir it into the lukewarm liquid with a pinch of the sugar first and wait until it foams.

Method

  1. Make the dough. Mix the flour, yeast, sugar and salt in a large bowl. Add the eggs, the lukewarm liquid and the vanilla or zest, and stir to a rough, shaggy dough. The liquid should feel just warm on your wrist, not hot, or it can kill the yeast.
  2. Knead in the fat. Knead by hand or with a dough hook for about 8–10 minutes, adding the butter, margarine or oil a little at a time. The dough starts sticky and becomes smooth, soft and slightly tacky. Resist adding lots of extra flour, which makes heavy doughnuts.
  3. First rise. Cover the bowl and leave it somewhere warm for 1 to 1½ hours, until the dough has doubled.
  4. Roll and cut. Turn the dough onto a lightly floured surface, roll it to about 1.5 cm (just over ½ inch) thick, and cut rounds about 7 cm (2¾ inches) across with a cutter or a glass. Gather and re-roll the scraps once.
  5. Second rise. Set each round on its own small square of floured baking paper, cover loosely, and leave for 30–45 minutes until visibly puffy. A gently pressed fingertip should leave a dent that slowly springs back.
  6. Heat the oil. Pour at least 5 cm (2 inches) of oil into a deep, heavy pot, so the doughnuts can float, and heat it to about 170 °C (340 °F).
  7. Fry. Lift each doughnut by its paper and slide it into the oil top side down (peel the paper away with tongs). Fry 3–4 at a time for about 1½–2 minutes per side, until deep golden. Drain on a wire rack.
  8. Fill and dust. Once they are cool enough to handle, poke a hole in the side of each one with a chopstick or skewer and wiggle it to make a pocket. Pipe in jam with a piping bag fitted with a narrow nozzle (or a zip-top bag with the corner snipped). Dust generously with powdered sugar just before serving.

Frying temperature: the part that matters most

Sufganiyot are thicker than latkes, so they need slightly cooler oil. Too hot and the outside darkens while the middle stays doughy. Too cool and the dough soaks up oil and turns greasy. A thermometer helps a lot here. Without one, fry a single scrap of dough first: it should rise to the surface within a few seconds and turn golden in about a minute.

Many bakers look for a pale ring around the middle of each doughnut. It appears when a well-risen doughnut floats high in the oil, and it is a good sign your proofing and temperature are right.

Deep frying needs care. Use a heavy pot no more than half full of oil, keep children well back, never leave the pot unattended, keep a lid nearby, and never put water on an oil fire.

Fillings and toppings

  • Classic: strawberry or raspberry jam. Smooth jam pipes more easily than chunky preserves; push chunky jam through a sieve first.
  • Pareve options: lemon curd made with oil or pareve margarine, pareve chocolate spread, or a pareve vanilla custard.
  • Dairy options: dulce de leche, chocolate-hazelnut spread (many brands contain milk), pastry cream, or a chocolate ganache glaze on top.
  • Toppings: powdered sugar, cinnamon sugar (roll them while still warm), or a simple glaze of powdered sugar and a little water or lemon juice.

Keeping sufganiyot kosher: dairy or pareve

If you plan to serve sufganiyot after a meat meal, make the pareve version with water or a plant milk and margarine or oil, use pareve equipment, and check that the jam, spreads and toppings are pareve too. Chocolate spreads and custards are the fillings most likely to contain dairy. As always, check that every ingredient carries a reliable kashrut certification.

Fried doughnuts are generally treated as mezonot, so the usual blessing is borei minei mezonot, though there are differing views when someone eats a meal-sized amount. This recipe uses 500 g of flour; if you scale up a lot for a crowd, ask your rabbi about separating challah, as dough meant for frying has its own rules.

Make-ahead and storage

  • Overnight dough: After kneading, cover the bowl and refrigerate it overnight instead of the first warm rise. Next day, roll and cut the cold dough (it’s easier to handle), then give the rounds a slightly longer second rise, often around an hour, until puffy.
  • Same day: Sufganiyot are best within a few hours of frying. Fill and dust them close to serving so the sugar doesn’t melt into the surface.
  • Freezing: Freeze unfilled, undusted doughnuts once completely cool. Thaw at room temperature, warm for a few minutes in a low oven, then fill and dust.

Troubleshooting

Raw or doughy in the middle

The oil was too hot, so the outside browned too fast, or the doughnuts were cut too thick. Lower the heat a little, and check one by breaking it open before frying the rest.

Greasy and heavy

The oil was too cool, or the pot was crowded. Fry fewer at a time and let the oil come back up to temperature between batches.

Flat or dense

The dough was under-proofed, or too much flour was added while kneading. Give the second rise its full time, and keep the dough soft.

Collapsed or wrinkled after frying

Usually a sign of over-proofing. If the rounds look very puffy and fragile, fry them sooner next time.

Can I bake them instead?

You can bake the risen rounds at around 190 °C (375 °F) for about 10–12 minutes, then brush with melted butter or margarine and roll in sugar. They’ll be good, soft filled buns, but not quite the same as fried sufganiyot.

Making them with kids

Children can help measure, knead, cut the circles, squeeze the jam in and do the sugar dusting, while an adult handles all the frying. It makes a good activity for one of the eight nights.
