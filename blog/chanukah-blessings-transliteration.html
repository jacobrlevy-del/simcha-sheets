Chanukah Blessings in Hebrew, Transliteration and English

Every night of Chanukah begins with the same short moment: the family gathers round the menorah, someone says the blessings, and the lights are kindled. If you didn’t grow up saying them, or you want the children to join in this year, this guide puts the blessings in one place, in Hebrew, in transliteration and in English, with a note on which nights each one is said. In 2026 the first candle is lit on Friday 4 December and the last on Friday 11 December.

Which blessings, which nights

BlessingWhen it’s said
1. Lehadlik ner (shel) ChanukahEvery night
2. She’asah nisimEvery night
3. ShehecheyanuFirst night only: Friday 4 December 2026

The blessings are said before lighting, since a blessing over a mitzvah is normally said just before doing it. If you weren’t able to light on the first night, Shehecheyanu is said on the first night you do light.

1. The blessing over the lights

בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו וְצִוָּנוּ לְהַדְלִיק נֵר שֶׁל חֲנֻכָּה.

Transliteration: Baruch atah Adonai, Eloheinu melech ha’olam, asher kid’shanu b’mitzvotav, v’tzivanu l’hadlik ner shel Chanukah.

English: Blessed are You, Lord our God, King of the universe, who has made us holy with His commandments and commanded us to kindle the Chanukah light.

Ashkenazi siddurim usually read ner shel Chanukah. Sephardi siddurim, following the Shulchan Aruch, and Chabad read ner Chanukah, without shel. The meaning is the same; use whichever your family’s siddur has.

2. The blessing for the miracles

בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, שֶׁעָשָׂה נִסִּים לַאֲבוֹתֵינוּ בַּיָּמִים הָהֵם בַּזְּמַן הַזֶּה.

Transliteration: Baruch atah Adonai, Eloheinu melech ha’olam, she’asah nisim la’avoteinu bayamim haheim baz’man hazeh.

English: Blessed are You, Lord our God, King of the universe, who performed miracles for our ancestors in those days, at this season.

3. Shehecheyanu (first night only)

בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה יְיָ אֱלֹהֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, שֶׁהֶחֱיָנוּ וְקִיְּמָנוּ וְהִגִּיעָנוּ לַזְּמַן הַזֶּה.

Transliteration: Baruch atah Adonai, Eloheinu melech ha’olam, shehecheyanu v’kiy’manu v’higi’anu laz’man hazeh.

English: Blessed are You, Lord our God, King of the universe, who has kept us alive, sustained us, and brought us to this season.

The Hebrew above writes God’s name as יְיָ, as many siddurim do. In a blessing it is pronounced Adonai. When practising with children outside of an actual blessing, many families say Hashem or Adoshem instead.

Step by step: lighting the menorah

  1. Set up the candles. Standing facing the menorah, put the first night’s candle in the far right holder. Each night, add the new candle to the left of the previous night’s candles, so the candles fill in from right to left.
  2. Light the shamash, the helper candle that sits apart from the others, often raised or set to one side. It is used to light the other candles.
  3. Say the blessings: two each night, three on the first night.
  4. Light the candles, newest first. In the common Ashkenazi practice you start with the candle added that night, on the left, and move to the right. Customs on the exact order vary, so follow your family’s practice.
  5. Put the shamash back in its place.
  6. Sing Haneirot Halalu and Ma’oz Tzur (below).

On the two Friday nights this year, all of this happens before the Shabbat candles are lit, with candles big enough to keep burning well into the night. Our guide to lighting the menorah on Friday night covers the timing in detail.

Who says the blessings

Customs differ here too. In many Ashkenazi homes, each member of the family lights their own menorah and says the blessings themselves, and children often have their own small menorah. In many Sephardi homes, one person lights for the whole household while everyone else listens and answers Amen. Either way, it’s a lovely moment for children to join in, even if they only say the last few words with you at first.

After lighting: Haneirot Halalu

Haneirot Halalu (“These lights”) is a short paragraph said after kindling; many begin it once the first light is lit, while lighting the rest. It opens הַנֵּרוֹת הַלָּלוּ אָנוּ מַדְלִיקִין (Haneirot halalu anu madlikin) and explains why we light: to remember the miracles and wonders, salvations and battles that God performed for our ancestors, in those days at this season. It goes on to say that for all eight days these lights are holy: we don’t use them for practical purposes like reading by them, only to look at them, so that we can give thanks and praise. That is also why the shamash is kept apart from the other lights.

Ma’oz Tzur

The best-known Chanukah song, sung after lighting in Ashkenazi homes. The first verse:

Ma’oz tzur y’shuati, l’cha na’eh l’shabei’ach.
Tikon beit t’filati, v’sham todah n’zabei’ach.
L’eit tachin matbei’ach, mitzar ham’nabei’ach,
Az egmor b’shir mizmor chanukat hamizbei’ach.

Meaning: Mighty Rock of my salvation, it is fitting to praise You. Restore my house of prayer, and there we will bring an offering of thanks. When You have prepared the slaughter of the blaspheming foe, then I will complete, with a song of psalm, the dedication of the altar.

The verses that follow tell of other times the Jewish people were saved: from Egypt, from Babylon, in the story of Purim, and finally from the Greeks in the Chanukah story. Many families sing just the first verse; some sing all of them, and the tunes vary from home to home. Many Sephardi families sing Psalm 30, Mizmor shir chanukat habayit, instead of or as well as Ma’oz Tzur.

Tips for learning the blessings with children

  • Start before Chanukah. Say the blessings together at bedtime for a week or so beforehand. By 4 December they’ll know the tune.
  • Learn the endings first. Small children can join in with “…ner shel Chanukah” and “…baz’man hazeh” long before they know the whole thing.
  • Keep a card by the menorah. A printed card with the Hebrew and the transliteration means nobody has to find the right page in a siddur in a dark room full of excited children.
  • Give everyone a job. Give younger children a job that keeps them close but safe: holding the card, counting the candles, or leading Amen.
