How to Play Dreidel: Rules, Letters and Simple Scoring

Dreidel is the game nearly every Jewish family plays on Chanukah. It's also the one where, every year, someone asks whether ה means half or nothing. This guide covers the whole game in a couple of minutes: what you need, what each letter means, what to do when the pot is odd or a player runs out, and a shorter version for young children. This year Chanukah begins on the evening of Friday 4 December 2026 and the eighth night is Friday 11 December.

What you need

  • A dreidel (one per player is nice, but one is enough).
  • Tokens: 10 to 15 each of chocolate coins, raisins, nuts, buttons or counters. Everyone starts with the same number.
  • A flat surface like a table or a hard floor. Carpet stops a dreidel quickly.
  • Optional: a scoreboard, so you can play several rounds and crown a champion.

The rules in five steps

  1. Every player puts one token into the middle. This is the pot.
  2. The youngest player usually spins first, then play goes round the circle.
  3. Spin the dreidel by its stem. When it falls, the letter facing up tells you what to do (see the table below).
  4. Whenever the pot is empty, or down to one token, everyone puts in one more.
  5. A player who runs out of tokens is out. The game ends when one player has everything, or at a time you agree before you start.

What the letters mean

A dreidel has a Hebrew letter on each of its four sides. The instructions come from Yiddish words that start with the same sounds.

LetterNameYiddishWhat you do
נNunNisht (nothing)Nothing happens. Pass the dreidel on.
גGimelGantz (all)Take the whole pot.
הHeiHalb (half)Take half the pot.
שShinShtel (put in)Put one token into the pot.

Together, the four letters stand for Nes Gadol Hayah Sham, “a great miracle happened there”, which refers to the Chanukah miracle in the Land of Israel.

Why Israeli dreidels have a פ

In Israel, the fourth letter is פ (Pei) instead of ש. It stands for Po, “here”, so the phrase becomes Nes Gadol Hayah Po, “a great miracle happened here”. In the game, פ works exactly like ש: put one token in. In Hebrew the dreidel is called a sevivon, from the word for “turn”. If you have both kinds at home, it's a nice way to start a conversation about the difference.

Simple scoring and house rules

The basic rules leave a few questions open, so families tend to make their own. Agree on these before the first spin and you'll avoid most arguments:

  • Odd pot on ה: the simplest rule is to round up, so with 5 tokens in the pot you take 3. Some families round down instead. Either works, as long as everyone knows which.
  • Shin: most people put in one token. Some families say “shin, shin, put one in” and put in three, which makes the game faster.
  • Running out: if you can't put a token in, you're out. For younger players, you could let them have one “rescue” from the bank instead.
  • Time limit: dreidel can go on for a long time. Set a timer for 15 or 20 minutes, and whoever holds the most tokens when it rings wins the round.
  • Several rounds: write each player's total after every round on a scoreboard, and add them up at the end of the night or of all eight nights.
  • Re-spin rule: if the dreidel falls off the table or lands leaning against something, spin again.

A shorter game for young children

Small children often lose interest before anyone wins. Try this version instead: give everyone 10 tokens, skip the ante after the first round, and play for exactly ten spins each. Whoever has the most tokens at the end wins. Let them call out the letter and the move themselves (“Gimel, I take it all!”). It's a painless way to learn the Hebrew letters.

Playing on the two Friday nights

This year the first and eighth nights of Chanukah fall on Shabbat. Money is not handled on Shabbat, so families who play on Friday night or Saturday use sweets, nuts or raisins rather than real coins. Customs on games during Shabbat vary, so ask your rabbi if you're unsure. Saturday night, 5 December, after Shabbat ends and the second candle is lit, is a good night for a proper dreidel tournament. Where does the game come from?

A popular story says that when studying Torah was banned under Greek rule, children kept a spinning top nearby so they could pretend to be playing if soldiers came. It's a lovely story to tell at the table, but it's a legend rather than documented history. Spinning-top games with lettered sides were played in Europe for centuries, and Jewish communities gave the letters a Chanukah meaning.

Fun facts for the table

  • In gematria (Hebrew letter values), נ ג ה ש adds up to 358 (50 + 3 + 5 + 300).
  • Try spinning the dreidel upside down on its stem. It's harder than it looks.
  • Ask the kids to design their own dreidel with four new rules, then play a round with it.
