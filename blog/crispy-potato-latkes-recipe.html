Crispy Potato Latkes: A Recipe That Actually Stays Crunchy

A good latke has lacy, shattering edges and a soft, savoury middle. A disappointing one is pale, greasy and limp. The difference rarely comes down to a secret ingredient. It comes down to two things: getting the water out and keeping the oil hot. This guide gives you a full pareve recipe, then covers make-ahead plans, reheating, troubleshooting and kosher topping ideas for both dairy and meat nights.

Ingredients (makes about 20)

  • 1 kg (about 2.2 lb) starchy potatoes, such as russet or Maris Piper
  • 1 medium onion
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3 tbsp plain flour or matzah meal
  • 1 tsp salt and ½ tsp black pepper
  • Neutral oil with a high smoke point (such as sunflower, canola or peanut) for frying

Starchy potatoes fry up crisper than waxy ones, and their starch helps the latkes hold together.

Method

  1. Grate the onion first. Use the coarse side of a box grater or a food processor’s grating disc. Then grate the potatoes straight into the same bowl and toss. The onion juice slows the potatoes from turning grey.
  2. Squeeze, then squeeze again. Tip everything into a clean tea towel, twist it closed and wring it out over a second bowl. Keep going until only a few drops come out. This is the single most important step for crisp latkes.
  3. Save the starch. Let the squeezed liquid sit for a few minutes. Pour off the water and you’ll find a pale paste at the bottom. That’s potato starch, and it’s a free, natural binder.
  4. Mix. Combine the potato and onion with the starch, eggs, flour, salt and pepper. Work quickly so the mixture doesn’t weep.
  5. Heat the oil. Pour about 1 cm (½ inch) of oil into a heavy pan and heat it to around 180 °C (350–360 °F). No thermometer? Drop in a shred of potato. It should sizzle immediately and briskly.
  6. Fry in small batches. Drop in heaped tablespoons, flatten them with the back of a spoon so the edges are thin and ragged, and leave plenty of space between them. Fry for 3–4 minutes per side until deep golden.
  7. Drain on a rack. A wire rack over a tray keeps the bottoms crisp. Paper towels work, but latkes can steam on them if they sit too long. Add a pinch of salt while they’re hot.

Getting the oil temperature right

Oil that’s too cool soaks into the potato and gives you heavy, greasy latkes. Oil that’s too hot browns the outside before the middle cooks. Aim for steady, lively bubbling around each latke. Every batch cools the pan, so let the oil recover for a minute between batches, and top it up as it runs low. If you see smoke, turn the heat down. Never leave hot oil unattended, keep a lid nearby, and never put water on an oil fire.

Make-ahead, freezing and reheating

Latkes taste best fresh, but frying for a crowd while the menorah is lit isn’t always realistic, especially on a busy Friday before Shabbat.

  • Same day: Fry a few hours ahead, cool on a rack and leave uncovered. Covering them traps steam and softens them.
  • Freezing: Cool completely, freeze in a single layer on a lined tray, then move them to a bag or container. They keep well for a few weeks.
  • Reheating: Spread them on a rack or baking tray in a hot oven, around 200 °C (400 °F). Fresh latkes need about 5–8 minutes; frozen ones can go straight in for roughly 10–15 minutes. Don’t thaw them first, and skip the microwave, which makes them soggy.

Troubleshooting

My latkes fall apart

Usually there’s too much water in the mix, or not enough binder. Squeeze harder, add the saved starch back, and add another spoonful of flour or matzah meal. Also leave each latke alone until the first side is properly set before flipping.

My latkes are soggy or greasy

The oil was too cool, or the pan was crowded. Fry fewer at a time and let the oil reheat between batches. Thin, flattened latkes crisp better than thick mounds.

My batter turned grey

Grated potato darkens when it meets the air. It’s harmless, but not pretty. Grate the onion first, mix and fry soon after grating, and if you’re working in batches, grate the second half of the potatoes later rather than letting it all sit.

They’re brown outside but raw inside

The oil is too hot or the latkes are too thick. Lower the heat slightly and flatten them more.

Dairy and pareve toppings for a kosher kitchen

Made with oil, eggs, flour and vegetables, this recipe is pareve, so it can sit alongside either a dairy or a meat meal. Check that every ingredient carries a reliable kashrut certification, and fry in pareve equipment if you plan to serve them with meat.

  • Pareve, for meat nights: applesauce, a sweet chilli or tomato relish, caramelised onions, or a dairy-free garlic and herb dip.
  • Dairy nights: sour cream with chives, Greek yoghurt, smoked salmon with cream cheese, or a little labneh with za’atar.

Many families also serve cheese latkes, a custom linked to the story of Yehudit. If you’re planning which Chanukah nights are dairy and which are meat, it helps to write it down before you shop.

Latkes are perfect for a family kitchen night. Children can wash the potatoes, help mix and set out toppings while an adult handles the grating and the hot oil.
